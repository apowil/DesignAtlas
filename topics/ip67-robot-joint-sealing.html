01 — Acceptance conditions

The two tests you have to pass

IP67 is not a design description. It is a pair of laboratory tests run on a new, de-energised sample. The numerals are tested independently and the result is pass/fail on ingress, not a leak rate.

The second numeral is not cumulative Passing IPx7 says nothing about jets. A robot that survives 30 minutes under a metre of still water can fail a hose in seconds, because immersion applies uniform hydrostatic pressure while a jet applies concentrated dynamic pressure that can lift a seal lip. Washdown cells need a dual rating — IP66/IP67, or IP69K for 80 °C, 8–10 MPa close-range cleaning. Specify both numerals separately.

02 — The governing physics

IP67 is a geometry problem, not a pressure problem

One metre of water is 9.8 kPa — about a tenth of an atmosphere. No seal in a robot is troubled by that number. What defeats IP67 is capillary paths, surface defects, assembly variation, and a pressure differential the robot generates inside itself that is larger than the test.

Fig. 1 — Pressure the seal must resist. The internal differential from a single thermal cycle outranks the immersion test itself.

Seal a joint cavity at 60 °C after a duty cycle, then let it cool to 20 °C. The trapped air follows the gas law at constant volume, so the internal pressure falls to roughly 89 kPa against 101 kPa outside. That is a 12 kPa depression — greater than the 9.8 kPa the immersion test pushes inward, and it acts in the same direction, pulling water past the lip seal rather than pressing it against its seat.

This is why sealed-but-unvented robots pass qualification and then fail in service. The lab test is 30 minutes on a cold, stable unit. The factory runs the arm hot, then a wash-down or a cold night pulls a vacuum through every seal on the machine, several times a day, for years.

Two consequences follow, and they drive everything in sections 04 to 07:

Therefore design for
  • Pressure equalisation on every sealed cavity
  • Consistent squeeze, not high squeeze
  • Short, simple, continuous seal perimeters
  • Surface finish and flatness as controlled dimensions
  • Tolerance stacks that hold squeeze at both limits
Not for
  • Pressure rating — 10 kPa is trivial
  • Anti-extrusion back-up rings
  • Heavier bolts or thicker flanges
  • Harder elastomers, which seal worse
  • A single dramatic seal that saves the design

03 — Assembly

Where the sealing happens

A robot is not one enclosure — it is a chain of rotating enclosures, and every joint is a moving interface that must hold a metre of head. Section through a typical cobot joint module. Hover or focus a callout to isolate it. Details A, B and C are drawn out in the following sections.

Fig. 2 — Section A–A through joint module. Adjacent parts hatched in opposing directions per ISO 128. Not to scale.

1

Rotary lip seal — detail B

The only seal that has to work while moving. Double-lip, grease-packed: outer lip excludes water and dust, inner lip retains gearbox grease.

2

Static O-ring in a gland — detail A

Every bolted parting line: end covers, service hatches, joint-to-link interfaces. Squeeze and fill are the two dimensions that matter.

3

Pressure-equalisation vent — detail C

The part people leave out, and the reason units pass on day one and fail at month six. Passes air, blocks liquid water.

4

Bonded seal washers on through-bolts

Any fastener breaking into the sealed cavity is a leak path through the thread helix. Blind holes, bonded washers, or anaerobic sealant — pick one rule.

5

Sealed connectors and gland entries

Rated at the mated assembly level. An unmated port is rated only when capped, and the cable core itself can wick water past a perfect gland.

6

Defence in depth on the electronics

Conformal coating, potted encoder cavity, potted connector back-ends. A small ingress event should be a maintenance item, not a dead joint.

7

The counterface, not just the seal

Half of lip-seal performance is the surface it runs on: hardened, plunge-ground, lead-free, concentric, corrosion-protected.

8

Labyrinth and slinger upstream

A non-contacting gap ahead of the lip takes the bulk of the water and debris. Costs no torque, buys most of the seal's life.

04 — Detail A

Static seals: squeeze, fill, finish

An O-ring seals because it is deformed, not because it is pressed hard. You are controlling two ratios and one surface. Get those right and static joints stop being a topic; get them wrong and no amount of bolt torque rescues them.

The rules that actually decide it

Squeeze must survive the tolerance stack. Nominal 25 % squeeze is meaningless if the worst-case stack of groove depth, flange flatness and part thickness lands you at 8 % on one corner. Run the stack at both extremes; the low limit must still exceed roughly 15 %, and the high limit must not overfill the groove.

Fill above 90 % is a failure mode, not a safety margin. The elastomer is effectively incompressible — it needs free volume to flow into. Thermal expansion, swell from cutting fluid, and the squeeze itself all consume that space. An overfilled groove forces the flange open and lifts the seal band.

Machining direction matters more than roughness number. A face-sealed flange turned on a lathe carries a fine spiral. That spiral is a helical channel across the seal band and will siphon water even at Ra 0.8. Face-mill, grind, or turn with a wiping insert, and specify "no continuous radial or spiral tool marks across the sealing band" as a note on the drawing.

Prefer a face seal to a radial gland in a bolted robot housing. Face seals set their squeeze from a machined depth against a hard metal-to-metal stop, so squeeze does not depend on bolt torque. Radial glands depend on a diametral fit and are far more sensitive to concentricity between two large castings.

Keep the perimeter continuous and gentle. Every corner is a stretch or compression discontinuity. Inside radii below about 3× cord diameter cause the ring to bunch and lift. Avoid splicing rings on site; use a single moulded ring or a form-in-place silicone bead dispensed by a robot with an inline flow check.

Cross-holes are the classic leak. A dowel hole, a bolt hole, or a drilled oil passage that breaks through the groove floor gives water a route under the seal band. Audit the CAD by cutting the groove floor as a surface and checking for any intersecting feature.

05 — Detail B

The rotary seal, and the surface under it

This is the only interface that is asked to seal while moving, and it is the one that consumes torque, generates heat and wears out. Half of its performance is not in the seal — it is in the counterface you grind for it to run on.

Tab. 2 — Counterface spec. Per ISO 6194 / DIN 3760 practice.

Lead is the silent killer. A turned shaft carries a microscopic helix. Under a rotating lip that helix acts as an Archimedes screw and pumps fluid in one direction — so the seal leaks in one direction of joint travel and looks fine in the other. Specify plunge grinding, and test both directions of rotation during qualification.

Never let the lip pass a sharp edge. Assembly damage is the single largest cause of infant-mortality leaks. Provide a proper lead-in chamfer, use an installation cone or a mandrel, lubricate the lip on assembly, and make it physically impossible to install the seal backwards — asymmetric case, or a shouldered bore.

Choose which side to seal. On the low-speed output the seal diameter is large, so drag torque is high but sliding speed and wear are low. On the high-speed motor shaft the diameter is small and drag is low, but sliding speed is 100× higher and life drops accordingly. Robot joints almost always seal at the output, because that is where the enclosure boundary physically is — accept the drag and compensate for it.

06 — Detail C

Pressure equalisation, the part that is always missing

A hydrophobic ePTFE membrane lets air move in and out of the cavity while blocking liquid water. It is a two-dollar part that decides whether your seals see 12 kPa of differential every duty cycle or none at all.

Fig. 5 — Detail C. Without equalisation, the cooling half of every duty cycle pulls the cavity below the ingress threshold.

What to specify. Water entry pressure comfortably above the test head — ≥ 20 kPa for IP67, higher for IP68 or IP69K duty. Airflow sized so the cavity can equalise faster than it heats: for a joint cavity of a few hundred millilitres and a normal thermal ramp, something in the region of 50–500 ml/min at 7 kPa is generous. Mounting is snap-in, threaded (M12×1.5 is common) or a self-adhesive patch over a drilled hole.

Where to put it. Somewhere that never sits in standing water through the arm's full range of motion, never takes a direct wash-down jet, and is not on the underside of a link where condensate collects. A small shroud or a recessed pocket is cheap insurance. One vent per sealed cavity — and a robot joint often has two cavities, motor side and gearbox side, if the lip seal separates them.

It also solves condensation. A perfectly sealed cavity assembled on a humid day contains humid air. Cool it below the dew point and you get liquid water inside an enclosure that never leaked. The vent lets that vapour leave during the warm part of the cycle. Assembling in a humidity-controlled area helps, but venting is what actually fixes it.

Oleophobic if there is oil mist. Standard hydrophobic membranes are defeated by oils and surfactants, which lower surface tension and wet the pores. In cells with cutting fluid or lubricant aerosols, specify the oleophobic grade — and protect the vent from direct spray regardless.

Do not "vent" with a labyrinth or a drain hole. That is just an unrated opening. The membrane is what makes the vent compatible with the IP claim, and the vent assembly itself must carry its own rating.

07 — Penetrations

Fasteners, connectors and the cable itself

Every hole through the enclosure wall is a decision. There are only three legitimate fastener strategies, and the cable that passes a perfectly sealed gland can still carry water into the joint along the inside of its own jacket.

Fig. 6 — Fastener strategies. Anaerobic thread sealant is a valid fourth option but depends on a process step, so it ranks below the two geometric solutions.

Connectors and cable entries

Rated only when mated. An M12 or push-pull connector carries its IP rating as a mated pair with the correct torque or latch engagement. Every unused port needs its sealing cap fitted, and the cap must be captive or the field will lose it. Treat caps as line items on the BOM and on the commissioning checklist, not as accessories.

Match the gland to the cable, not to the hole. Cable glands seal over a clamping range. A jacket at the bottom of the range compresses unevenly, and an oval or ribbed jacket never seals at all. Specify round, smooth-jacketed cable, and confirm the actual jacket OD from the cable datasheet rather than the nominal.

Give it a drip loop and a strain relief. Water runs down a cable and collects at the entry. Route so the cable approaches from below, and take the mechanical load in a clamp ahead of the gland, so the gland only seals and never carries tension.

08 — Failure geography

Where robot arms actually leak

Ranked by how often the interface is the one that fails a 30-minute immersion. The joints usually are not the problem — the interfaces that engineering treats as somebody else's part are.

Fig. 8 — Ingress zones, ranked.

  1. ATool flangeElectrical, pneumatic and sometimes fluid pass-throughs crossing a rotating interface, plus a customer-designed gripper bolted to it. Your rating stops at your flange face — the end effector is not covered by your certificate, and integrators routinely assume it is. Say so explicitly in the manual.
  2. BBase connector panelPower, fieldbus, safety I/O and air, all entering at floor level where water collects, plus unmated spare ports left uncapped in the field.
  3. CWrist clusterTwo or three axes packed into the smallest housings, so seal diameters are small, cavities are tiny and there is no room for a labyrinth or a vent.
  4. DService and brake-release portsCovers that get opened in the field and reclosed by someone who does not know the O-ring is a single-use part or that torque matters.
  5. EJoint parting linesThe best-behaved interface on the arm, because it is designed once and never opened. Static seals in machined glands rarely fail if the vent is present.

09 — Verification and process

You cannot dunk every arm

Immersion is the type test. Production needs something that takes thirty seconds, catches the same defects, and can be run on a sub-assembly before it is buried inside a finished robot.

Fig. 9 — Pressure decay. Settle time exists because filling heats the gas; measure only after it stabilises, or the thermal transient swamps the leak signal.

  1. Correlate the decay limit to immersion, do not guess itBuild master parts with calibrated leaks — a laser-drilled orifice of known size — immersion-test them, and set the production threshold from where pass and fail actually separate. A decay limit chosen from intuition either passes leakers or scraps good parts.
  2. Pressurise, never evacuatePressure pushes seals against their seats in the same direction that immersion does not. If you want a harsher test, that asymmetry is exactly why some teams also run a vacuum decay — it reproduces the thermal-cooldown condition from section 02.
  3. Give the vent a plug fixtureYou cannot leak-test a vented cavity. The fixture must positively seal over the vent, and the process must guarantee the plug is removed afterwards. Poka-yoke it; a shipped robot with a blocked vent is worse than one with no vent at all.
  4. Immersion-test after thermal shock, not beforeThe field condition is a hot arm hit with cold water. Qualification should follow the same order: thermal cycling, then immersion, then a second immersion after the endurance run. Testing a virgin cold sample flatters the design.
  5. Run both directions of joint rotationMachining lead on the counterface pumps one way. A seal that passes clockwise can leak counter-clockwise, and a robot joint does both all day.
  6. Test the seal you will ship, at the tolerance extremesBuild qualification samples deliberately at minimum squeeze and maximum groove fill. Nominal parts hide the design's real margin.
  7. Re-verify after serviceThe rating belongs to the assembly, not the drawing. Define which seals are single-use, publish the torque sequence, and require a decay test after any field opening of a sealed cavity.

10 — Consequences

What sealing costs you elsewhere

IP67 is not free performance. Every one of these shows up in the datasheet of a sealed robot compared with its open equivalent, and each is worth pricing into the spec before it is committed.

ConsequenceOrder of magnitudeWhy, and what to do about it
Thermal deratingContinuous torque ↓ A sealed cavity has no convection. All motor and drive loss must conduct out through the housing wall, so the aluminium structure becomes the heatsink. Expect thicker walls, a deliberate thermal path from stator to housing, and a lower continuous duty rating than the same actuator in an open frame.
Seal dragParasitic torque + heat Every lip seal takes torque, and that loss becomes heat exactly where you least want it. It also corrupts current-based force estimation — a real issue if the arm does collision detection or force control without joint torque sensors. Characterise the drag across temperature and compensate it, or budget for torque sensing.
Wear-limited lifeType test ≠ lifetime The certificate is a test on a new sample. Lip seals wear, elastomers take compression set, and grease migrates. Define a seal service interval, and re-verify IP after the endurance test rather than only before it.
Chemical compatibilityElastomer selection NBR is the default and is attacked by several common sanitisers and cutting fluids. EPDM handles steam and caustics but swells in oil. FKM covers most solvents and higher temperatures. Choose against the customer's actual cleaning chemistry — see Tab. 3.
ServiceabilityField repair ↓ Every sealed interface is one a technician can compromise. Minimise the number of covers that ever need opening, make single-use seals visually obvious, ship spare seal kits with the service part, and put the torque sequence on a label inside the cover.
Assembly disciplineProcess capability Sealing is a process, not a drawing note. Controlled torque and sequence, single-use O-rings, gland cleanliness, lip lubrication on install, and a documented handling rule for the ground counterface — a fingernail scratch on that surface is a failed unit.
