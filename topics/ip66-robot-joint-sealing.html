01 — Acceptance conditions

The same jet test, four times the pressure

IP66 keeps IP65's dust-tight first numeral and swaps the 6.3 mm low-pressure nozzle for a 12.5 mm nozzle at roughly 100 kPa — about the pressure a domestic pressure washer produces, not an industrial one. It is the rating for equipment that gets hosed hard, outdoors or in a heavy-duty wash-down cell, and it is very commonly specified as a dual rating with IP67.

02 — Why the dual rating exists

66/67 covers two failure modes IP67 alone doesn't

The IP67 sheet already flags this: immersion applies uniform hydrostatic pressure, a jet applies concentrated dynamic pressure, and the second numeral is not cumulative. IP67 alone says nothing about whether a lip seal can be lifted locally by a jet — IP66 alone says nothing about a joint left standing in a puddle. Outdoor and heavy-industrial arms routinely need both, specified and tested as a pair: IP66/IP67.

Test both, don't infer either A design that passes the 100 kPa jet does not automatically pass 30 minutes of static immersion, and vice versa — they load the seal geometry differently (Sec. 02 of the IP65 sheet has the full mechanism). If the spec says IP66/IP67, qualification means running both tests on the same sample, not picking the one that sounds harder.

03 — Detail design

What 100 kPa asks of the seal that 30 kPa doesn't

Lip seal margin, not lip seal type. The same double-lip construction from the IP67 detail still applies — but at 100 kPa local impingement, garter-spring tension and radial interference deserve to be checked against the seal maker's own pressure-capability curve, not just assumed from a catalogue part number picked for the shaft diameter alone.

Gland flatness matters more. A jet finds the one high point on a face seal where squeeze has locally dropped below spec, in a way a slow static head is less likely to. Re-check the tolerance-stack squeeze analysis from IP67 Sec. 04 at its worst corner, not its nominal.

Connector and cable entries take the most direct hit. A jet swept across a base panel loads every gland and cap at full test pressure, repeatedly, over the equipment's life — not just once at qualification. Captive caps and a genuine clamping-range match to the cable jacket (IP67 Sec. 07) stop being nice-to-haves.

The vent still needs a shroud. A membrane rated only for splash or a light jet is a liability at 100 kPa; specify the vent's own ingress rating against the wash-down pressure it will actually see, and mount it out of the direct sweep path.

04 — Deployment

IP66 usually means outdoor, and outdoor adds UV and thermal cycling

Rating the enclosure for water is necessary but not sufficient once the deployment is outdoors. Two consequences follow that a pure IP-code reading misses.

  1. UVElastomer degradationNBR and standard EPDM chalk and crack under sustained UV; an outdoor IP66/67 joint should specify a UV-stabilised compound or a compound inherently resistant to it (silicone, UV-stable FKM), not just the pressure-rated Shore hardness from Tab. 3 of the IP67 sheet.
  2. Thermal rangeWider cycling, bigger vent swingAn outdoor enclosure sees a larger daily and seasonal temperature range than an indoor cell, which means the pressure-equalisation vent has to pass more air, more often — size it against the actual outdoor delta-T, not the 60→20 °C duty-cycle figure the IP67 sheet uses as its indoor baseline.

05 — Verification

Production testing is unchanged — the correlation isn't

The pressure-decay rig from the IP67 sheet is the same rig here. What changes is the reject threshold: it must be correlated against calibrated-leak master parts pushed to failure under the 100 kPa jet, not the 9.8 kPa immersion head — a decay limit borrowed from an IP67 line will pass IP66 leakers that a jet would have caught.
